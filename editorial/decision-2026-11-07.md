Referee: 5

Recommendation: Major Revision

Comments:
The revised manuscript, “Governing Data Flows in Servitised Manufacturing Systems,” addresses an important issue in enterprise information management: the governance of operational data as they move across organisational boundaries in digitally enabled product–service systems.
The revision introduces several meaningful improvements. In particular, the manuscript now distinguishes the conceptual flow-governance framework from the descriptive analysis of monetary intermediate transactions. The proposed five conditional propositions, the distinction between latent governance gaps and realised failures, and the accompanying research agenda provide a clearer theoretical contribution.
The revised analysis also demonstrates greater methodological caution by acknowledging that OECD input–output data do not directly measure information exchange or governance effectiveness.
Nevertheless, several issues remain to be addressed.
1. Consolidation of the revised manuscript
The most immediate concern is the presentation of the R2 document. The submitted version contains extensive overlapping passages from earlier and revised formulations, duplicated headings, inconsistent section numbering and visible editing remnants.
For example, the structured abstract combines different versions of the purpose, methodology and findings. Similar problems occur in the introduction, theoretical discussion and conclusions.
The authors should provide a clean, fully consolidated manuscript in which all superseded material has been removed and the revised argument can be assessed without ambiguity.
2. Distinctiveness of the conceptual contribution
The revised manuscript more clearly positions the inter-organisational data flow as the unit of governance analysis. This is a useful perspective, particularly for examining downstream obligations, derived data and fragmented authority.
However, the authors should retain a concise and explicit comparison with established approaches to ecosystem governance, federated data governance, data sovereignty and usage control.
The contribution should be framed as a complementary analytical perspective rather than as a replacement for these existing frameworks.
3. Theoretical propositions and their empirical status
The five propositions are more carefully formulated than a general claim that increasingly complex product–service systems necessarily require more elaborate governance structures.
The inclusion of boundary conditions and potential falsifying evidence is particularly valuable.
Nevertheless, the propositions remain theoretical expectations derived from interpretive synthesis. Their validity is not demonstrated by the descriptive OECD analysis.
The authors should consistently maintain this distinction throughout the abstract, discussion and conclusions.
4. Interpretation of monetary input–output transactions
The revised analysis provides useful economic context for relationships between manufacturing and business-services industries.
However, monetary deliveries cannot establish the existence, volume, direction or governance requirements of accompanying data flows.
The authors now acknowledge this limitation, which substantially improves the interpretation. They should ensure that all remaining references to coordination intensity or governance pressure are expressed as hypotheses or possible implications rather than empirical findings.
5. Sensitivity to sectoral classification
The revised industry-level decomposition reveals that the aggregate results depend substantially on the definition of the business-services group.
The reported asymmetry is positive for the broad group but becomes negative for information and communication industries considered separately.
This is an important finding because it shows that the aggregate relationship is not representative of every type of knowledge-intensive service.
The discussion should give this sensitivity appropriate prominence and avoid treating the broad business-services category as a homogeneous proxy for digitally intensive services.
6. Temporal relevance of the empirical illustration
The analysis relies on the 2015 OECD ICIO matrix.
Although this dataset is suitable for illustrating the structure of historical inter-industry transactions, its reference year predates important developments in industrial data spaces, cloud platforms, artificial intelligence and recent data-governance regulation.
The authors should clearly explain that the analysis provides a historical economic illustration and cannot establish contemporary trends or the current scale of digital coordination requirements.
7. Operationalisation of the proposed framework
The proposed flow register and the research agenda are useful additions.
To strengthen their practical relevance, the authors should clarify how an organisation would determine whether a legitimate governance requirement is adequately covered by existing contractual, relational or technical mechanisms.
A concise description of the assessment procedure, including the evidence needed to identify an unmet requirement, would make the framework easier to apply in future empirical studies.
8. Relationship between the conceptual and descriptive contributions
The manuscript now explicitly recognises that the conceptual framework does not depend on the OECD analysis.
This distinction should guide the final organisation of the paper.
The authors should avoid suggesting that the economic analysis validates the theoretical propositions. Instead, the two contributions should be presented as a conceptual explanation of flow-governance requirements and a separate descriptive account of the economic environment in which some relevant inter-organisational relationships occur.
Overall assessment
The revised manuscript demonstrates a more coherent theoretical contribution and a more appropriately limited interpretation of its empirical material. The five propositions and the associated research agenda provide a useful foundation for subsequent investigation.
The remaining concerns primarily involve consolidation of the revised document, precision in distinguishing conceptual claims from empirical evidence, and clearer operationalisation of the proposed framework.
Addressing these issues would substantially improve the manuscript's clarity, credibility and suitability for publication.

Referee: 6

Recommendation: Major Revision

Comments:
The manuscript examines inter-organisational data governance in servitised manufacturing. It develops a flow-governance framework centred on governance demand, capacity and latent gaps, supplemented by a descriptive analysis of manufacturing–business services transactions using OECD ICIO data. The revised manuscript addresses several concerns raised in the previous review by revising the relationship between product–service system (PSS) types and governance mechanisms, introducing conditional propositions, extending the industry-level analysis and clarifying the limitations of monetary transaction data. These revisions improve the conceptual clarity and scope of the study.
However, substantive concerns remain regarding the framework's theoretical contribution, the identification of governance gaps and the relationship between the conceptual and descriptive components. Several definitions and propositions also require further clarification. The following comments focus on these remaining issues.
1. The theoretical contribution of flow governance requires further clarification. Section 2.2 acknowledges existing research on inter-organisational data control, usage-policy inheritance and data sovereignty. However, it remains unclear what the proposed mechanisms of downstream obligation attenuation explain beyond these established perspectives.
2. The criteria for identifying a latent governance gap need to be more precise. Section 3.2 defines legitimate requirements as arising from legal obligations, agreements or specified operational conditions, yet also treats the absence of contractual provisions concerning derived data as a potential gap. Missing provisions do not necessarily indicate unmet obligations.
3. The contribution of the ICIO analysis to the conceptual framework remains insufficiently established. Although the additional industry decompositions improve the descriptive analysis, the revised manuscript explicitly acknowledges that monetary transactions do not directly measure data flows or governance conditions. It remains unclear how these descriptive findings contribute to the paper’s overall argument beyond addressing the second research question. For example, the reversal of the asymmetry index for information and communication industries is reported without explaining its implications for the framework.
4. The boundaries of an inter-organisational data flow require clearer specification. Section 3.1 includes transfers, transformations, onward sharing and derivatives within the same analytical unit, but does not establish when related activities constitute one flow or multiple flows. For instance, sensor data may simultaneously support maintenance, performance-based payments and model training, while derived models may remain in use after the original transfer ends. Different boundary choices could affect the identification of participants, transformations and governance requirements.
5. Proposition 1 requires a more precise theoretical derivation and measurement strategy. An additional recipient may increase the number of points at which an existing obligation must be fulfilled without necessarily increasing the number or stringency of governance requirements. Similarly, different transformations may create different safeguarding needs. The proposition should distinguish the number of requirements, their stringency and the burden of implementation. In addition, Table I proposes coding requirements from agreements and flow documentation, potentially conflating governance demand with the completeness of existing documentation.
6. The fifth assumption in Proposition 2 requires further justification. The condition that neither party benefits from undetected deviation substantially limits the scope for opportunistic behaviour, raising questions about the proposition's explanatory value. The author should explain why this assumption is necessary in addition to low-cost verification, specifiable uses and contractual enforceability. It should also be clarified whether the proposition describes a practically identifiable class of exchange arrangements or an idealised theoretical benchmark, as this distinction affects its interpretation and contribution.
7. The governance role of the proposed flow register needs clearer specification. Table III assigns coordination, assurance and escalation responsibilities to designated participants, but the framework itself identifies fragmented authority as a central governance challenge. A register owner may lack the contractual authority to obtain downstream records, enforce cooperation or resolve conflicting obligations.
